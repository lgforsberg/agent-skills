# Writing modes

Read the section that matches the task. Use only as much of it as helps.

## Technical

For engineers, architects, operators, and technical evaluators.

Focus on architecture, components, interfaces, data flow, protocols,
dependencies, operational behavior, inputs and outputs, security boundaries,
deployment, constraints, and failure behavior.

Use the terminology the audience is expected to know. Do not explain those
concepts unless the reader is mixed.

## Product

For customers, partners, product teams, and industry audiences.

Explain what the product is, who it is for, what problem it addresses, what it
does, how it works at an appropriate level, why the capability matters, and how
it fits into existing workflows.

Connect technical capabilities to practical outcomes. Do not turn the text into
generic marketing copy.

## Executive

For management and decision-makers.

Focus on purpose, business relevance, risk, operational impact, strategic value,
major capabilities, important constraints, and implementation implications.

Avoid implementation detail unless it materially affects the decision. Do not
dumb down the subject.

## Sales

For prospects and commercial conversations.

Be persuasive through specificity: the customer's problem, practical outcomes,
differentiation, capabilities, integration, operational value, and reduction of
complexity, cost, effort, or risk where those are supported.

Do not create urgency artificially, oversell, or hide meaningful limitations.
Credibility is more valuable than aggressive persuasion.

A useful shape is problem → capability → practical effect → evidence or detail.
When differentiation is known, explain the actual difference.

Weak:

> Unlike traditional solutions, our unique platform delivers unparalleled
> flexibility.

Better:

> Rules are configured independently from the underlying detection services,
> allowing customers to change case handling without modifying the ingestion
> pipeline.

## Explainer

For knowledgeable readers who are not specialists in this technology.

Explain what something is, why it exists, how it works, where it fits, and why
it matters. Introduce terminology when it becomes useful. Prefer concrete
examples over abstract definitions.

## Service description

For professional or managed services.

Make clear what the service does, its scope, the intended customer, inputs,
activities, outputs, responsibilities, dependencies, customer responsibilities,
delivery model, exclusions where relevant, and what is ongoing versus one-time.

Avoid "we provide comprehensive support." Describe what actually happens.

## System description

For a platform, infrastructure, internal system, or technical solution.

A useful order, used only where it helps:

1. Purpose
2. Scope
3. Major components
4. Data or request flow
5. External dependencies
6. Interfaces
7. Persistence
8. Security boundaries
9. Operational behavior
10. Constraints

Do not force every system into this template.

## Website / brochure

For public pages, brochures, product sheets, and concise marketing material.

Lead with what the thing is, who it helps, and what it enables. Then give enough
substance to establish credibility.

Prefer concrete capability descriptions to marketing adjectives. Do not require
the reader to decode internal terminology before understanding the value.
